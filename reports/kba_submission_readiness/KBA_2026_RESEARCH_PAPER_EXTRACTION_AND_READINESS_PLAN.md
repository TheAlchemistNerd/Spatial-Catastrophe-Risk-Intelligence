## 1. What the official KBA brief requires

The official call asks for high-quality analytical and empirical work that generates implementable recommendations. A proposal may address one or several sub-themes, must be no more than five pages, and must include a 300–500-word abstract, clear motivation, testable hypotheses and a brief methodology [1]. Selected conference papers may be reviewed for the KBA Working Paper Series and its EconStor collection. The series describes its papers as work in progress intended to stimulate discussion and advance banking-industry knowledge [2].

The SCRI paper fits the brief in three linked ways:

1. **Primary alignment—risk identification and management.** It measures how floods and droughts enter credit risk through borrower income, production, business interruption, collateral condition, market access and recovery time.
2. **Secondary alignment—bank credit-pricing dynamics.** It tests whether spatial physical-risk features improve the estimation of borrower risk and expected loss. Any pricing analysis should be presented as a governed application of validated risk estimates within the bank’s own model. Kenya’s revised Risk-Based Credit Pricing Model uses KESONIA plus a premium that includes the borrower’s risk profile and other lending costs [3].
3. **Methodological contribution.** It compares a transparent bank-risk baseline with a point-in-time SCRI challenger that uses hazard intensity, footprint, duration, exposure, vulnerability and response evidence.

Recent KBA publications indicate the expected scale and style. Climate-related working papers examined here are about 32–36 pages and approximately 8,500–10,600 extracted words. They generally combine an abstract, introduction, Kenyan stylised facts, literature review, methodology, data, empirical results, policy implications and references. KBA’s associated policy briefs can be two pages and foreground key messages, evidence and proposed action [4]–[7]. The target SCRI paper should therefore be an 8,000–10,000-word research manuscript plus a two-page policy brief, rather than a shortened version of the entire book-length white paper.

## 2. The research contribution and its novelty

The strongest contribution becomes visible when SCRI is positioned beside existing KBA research.

Kimundi and Wambui study the time-varying transmission of temperature and precipitation through climate-policy-relevant sectors to a composite banking-stability index, using quarterly Kenyan data and VAR, impulse-response and time-varying Granger-causality methods [4]. Odongo, Misati, Kageha and Wamalwa use quarterly data for 35 banks over 2009–2021, dynamic panel estimation and temperature stress scenarios to examine bank stability and non-performing-loan risk [5]. Wamalwa, Kamau, Odongo and Misati use quarterly 2000–2023 data and an autoregressive distributed-lag model to examine how bank credit, temperature and precipitation relate to cereal production [6].

SCRI can extend this literature from national or sector-level climate indicators to **spatially resolved, event-linked and point-in-time banking exposure**. The proposed unit of analysis is a loan, borrower, collateral asset, branch portfolio, or a carefully aggregated county-sector-quarter cell. The paper asks whether knowing that a borrower or collateral asset intersected a flood footprint of a given depth and duration—or experienced a drought state of a given persistence—improves prediction of arrears, restructuring, stage migration, default, cure or collateral recovery beyond conventional macroeconomic, sectoral and county variables.

This is timely. CBK’s 2021 Guidance on Climate-Related Risk Management asks institutions to identify material physical risk at portfolio, counterparty and, where appropriate, transaction level. It specifically points to business and asset location, supply-chain disruption and collateral valuation, and encourages climate stress testing, quantitative indicators and integration through the credit life cycle [8]. The Kenya Green Finance Taxonomy adds a complementary investment question: an adaptation activity should be supported by a physical climate-risk assessment, appropriate geographic and temporal scales, uncertainty analysis, measurable outcomes and continuing monitoring [9]. International banking guidance similarly emphasises sufficiently granular data, geospatial characteristics, exposure mapping, damage functions and links to conventional credit-risk measures [10]–[13].

The paper’s contribution can therefore be stated in one sentence:

> It tests whether a Kenya-specific, point-in-time catastrophe evidence layer adds measurable predictive and decision value to bank credit-risk identification, while showing how the same evidence can support resilience finance and climate-risk disclosure.

## 4. Proposed research questions and hypotheses

The primary research question should be:

> Does the addition of spatially and temporally resolved flood and drought intelligence improve the out-of-sample identification and calibration of climate-related credit risk in Kenyan bank portfolios relative to conventional sectoral, county and macro-climate indicators?

Secondary questions are:

- Through which channels—revenue interruption, production loss, market access, collateral damage, household income or recovery delay—does the effect emerge?
- Does insurance, verified adaptation or access to anticipatory finance moderate distress and accelerate cure?
- How does predictive value vary by sector, county, borrower size, livelihood system and observation coverage?
- How could validated information support credit monitoring, portfolio concentration, scenario analysis, expected credit loss, risk-based pricing and resilience-finance origination?

The proposal can state five testable hypotheses:

- **H1:** Adding SCRI flood and drought features improves out-of-sample prediction and calibration of borrower distress relative to a conventional credit-risk baseline.
- **H2:** Hazard intensity, footprint and duration affect credit outcomes through observable cash-flow, downtime, production and collateral channels.
- **H3:** Insurance coverage, functioning resilience assets and documented early action reduce conditional loss severity or shorten recovery time.
- **H4:** Fine-resolution exposure data changes estimated geographic and sectoral concentration relative to county-average climate variables.
- **H5:** Model performance and uncertainty vary with borrower segment and data coverage, making coverage diagnostics an essential part of fair and reliable use.

## 5. Data and empirical design

### 5.1 Three feasible data tiers

**Tier A—loan-level partner study** is the preferred design. One or more banks would supply pseudonymised loan records with an approved spatial key. Essential fields include facility and borrower identifiers, sector, borrower type, origination and maturity, balance, arrears or days past due, restructuring, stage or default status, write-off, cure, collateral type and location, insurance status, and recovery outcomes. Exact personal identifiers remain with the bank. The research environment receives the minimum geography needed for hazard linkage.

**Tier B—aggregated bank–sector–county panel** supports a credible alternative. Outcomes can include outstanding credit, new lending, average pricing where available, non-performing loans, restructuring and provisioning by bank, sector, county and quarter. Aggregation reduces identification risk and may ease participation, although it also reduces the ability to separate exposure, vulnerability and borrower behaviour.

**Tier C—public-data methodological demonstrator** can support a late Methodological Session. It would combine public or licensed hazard and economic information with a synthetic loan portfolio or a fully disclosed aggregate outcome. This route can demonstrate the complete SCRI pipeline and validation protocol. The empirical Working Paper would follow when partner outcomes are available.

### 5.2 Hazard and contextual inputs

Flood features should include rainfall accumulation and anomaly, river or drainage level where available, inundation probability, observed extent, modelled depth band, event duration, access disruption and event-recovery time. Drought features should include precipitation deficit, vegetation condition, soil moisture, water-point condition, livestock or crop indicators, market access and duration in each drought state. Potential sources include KMD, WRA, NDMA, Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2, CHIRPS rainfall, ERA5 reanalysis, county records and governed community observations. Each variable needs an event time, publication or knowledge time, spatial support, revision history and quality flag so the model only uses information available at the prediction date.

Macroeconomic and bank controls should include lending conditions, KESONIA or the applicable benchmark, inflation, sector output, exchange-rate and county economic indicators, facility characteristics and borrower repayment history. These controls prevent the hazard signal from absorbing ordinary credit-cycle variation.

### 5.3 Estimation strategy

The empirical design should contain a transparent baseline and a challenger.

The **baseline** can be a regularised logistic, discrete-time survival or stage-transition model using borrower history, facility terms, macroeconomic variables, sector and coarse geography. The **SCRI challenger** adds point-in-time hazard, exposure, vulnerability, insurance, resilience and recovery variables. A hierarchical structure can pool information across counties and sectors while allowing local differences. Flood and drought should use separate physical features even when they feed the same credit outcome.

For the 2024 flood or another well-observed event, an event-study design can compare outcome paths for affected and credibly comparable unaffected exposures, subject to pre-trend and overlap diagnostics. Drought requires a duration-aware design: distributed lags, state duration or survival models can trace accumulated livelihood and business effects. Predictive improvement and causal attribution should be reported as separate findings. The paper can claim predictive value from honest holdout performance; it can claim intervention effect only where the comparison design supports that conclusion.

Performance measures should include calibration curves, calibration slope and intercept, Brier score, log loss, discrimination, sensitivity at an operational review capacity, lead time, stability across time and geography, and uncertainty. Bank-value measures can include earlier identification of at-risk exposure, change in expected credit loss, concentration revealed, and workload required per correctly escalated account. PD, LGD and EAD should be analysed separately before combination. A pricing illustration may then show how a validated change in borrower risk could enter the bank’s governed premium, with affordability, conduct and geographic-fairness review.

Robustness work should test alternative hazard definitions, spatial buffers, time lags, drought thresholds, missing-location treatment, flood-map error, event exclusions, county holdouts and pre/post-event windows. Subgroup calibration should be examined by borrower size, sector, county, gender where lawfully available, and digital observation coverage. A model that gains average discrimination while losing calibration for remote or sparsely reported areas should remain a challenger until the coverage problem is resolved.

## 6. Target paper and submission package

The full paper should use a compact structure that matches KBA’s published style:

1. **Abstract and key contribution**—300–350 words.
2. **Introduction**—the Kenyan borrower–bank–catastrophe problem, research question, contribution and findings.
3. **Kenyan context and related literature**—physical-risk transmission, existing KBA evidence, CBK guidance and the gap filled by granular data.
4. **Conceptual framework**—hazard → exposure and vulnerability → business/collateral effect → PD, LGD and EAD → bank action; resilience and insurance as moderators.
5. **Data and construction of SCRI variables**—sample, sources, linkage, timing, descriptive statistics and governance.
6. **Empirical methods**—baseline, challenger, event or duration design, validation and hypotheses.
7. **Results and robustness**—effect estimates, predictive uplift, calibration, concentrations, heterogeneity and limitations.
8. **Banking, resilience-finance and policy implications**—credit life cycle, risk pricing, disclosure, project origination and community access.
9. **Conclusion**—the answer to the research question and the next validation step.

The KBA-facing package should contain:

- a five-page proposal with 300–500-word abstract;
- a biography of no more than two pages;
- the 8,000–10,000-word full paper;
- a two-page policy brief;
- a 12–15-slide conference presentation;
- a data dictionary and methodological appendix; and
- a reproducibility and review statement describing data restrictions, code availability and independent checks.

## 7. Detailed implementation sequence

### Track A: immediate KBA contact—2 to 5 working days

1. Freeze the recommended title, primary question and flood–drought scope.
2. Prepare a one-page concept note containing the contribution, data route, intended bank value and current research status.
3. Prepare a 300–500-word abstract and a short professional biography.
4. Email `research@kba.co.ke` to request the best current route: late Methodological Session consideration, Working Paper submission after the conference, or guidance for the next cycle.
5. If KBA offers a September slot, present a clearly labelled methodological demonstrator and research protocol. Use only completed evidence as results.

**Gate A:** written confirmation from KBA of the submission route, format and timing.

### Track B: empirical Working Paper—12 to 16 weeks from data access

**Weeks 1–2: question and evidence freeze.** Complete a focused literature review, claim ledger and paper outline. Define the prediction date, outcome, sample unit, horizons and primary estimand. Register hypotheses and performance measures internally before modelling.

**Weeks 2–4: banking partnership and data specification.** Secure a data owner, lawful research basis, minimum geography, access controls and data dictionary. Conduct a sample-size and event-overlap assessment before promising loan-level inference.

**Weeks 3–6: hazard feature construction.** Reconstruct selected flood and drought periods, build point-in-time features, document spatial joins and create quality/coverage indicators. Independent hazard review confirms that variables represent the underlying peril appropriately.

**Weeks 5–7: analytical panel.** Join hazard and banking data in the controlled environment; normalise facilities and outcomes; document exclusions, missingness and survivorship; create train, validation, temporal holdout and geographic holdout samples.

**Weeks 7–9: baseline and challenger modelling.** Estimate the transparent baseline first, then the SCRI features and any hierarchical challenger. Record versioned metrics, feature stability, calibration and operational thresholds.

**Weeks 9–11: causal and financial translation.** Where design permits, estimate event or duration effects. Translate outputs into PD, LGD, EAD, expected-loss and portfolio-concentration views. Test insurance and resilience moderators. Keep the credit-pricing application as a scenario tied to the bank’s governance.

**Weeks 11–13: robustness and review.** Complete sensitivity, subgroup, missing-data, spatial and temporal tests. Obtain review from a banking credit-risk practitioner, econometrician, hazard scientist, actuary, data-protection specialist and community/financial-inclusion practitioner.

**Weeks 13–16: authoring and submission.** Draft the full paper from verified tables and figures, then prepare the policy brief and presentation. Reconcile every result across text, tables and appendices; verify every reference URL and access date; obtain final editorial and mathematical review.

- **Gate 5—banking usefulness:** a bank reviewer can trace results into monitoring, concentration, expected loss, stress testing or resilience finance.
- **Gate 6—publication quality:** methods, results, citations, equations, figures, policy brief and presentation reconcile after independent review.

Use the September opportunity to open the institutional conversation, and use the Working Paper route to deliver the strongest research. The immediate note to KBA should lead with the precise paper question, its novelty relative to KBA’s existing climate-and-bank-stability work, the flood–drought empirical design and the availability of a mature SCRI research architecture. The result will be recognisably derived from SCRI while speaking directly to the banking audience: how a bank can see physical risk sooner, measure it more locally, connect it to conventional credit risk, finance adaptation and evaluate whether resilience changed the borrower’s outcome.
