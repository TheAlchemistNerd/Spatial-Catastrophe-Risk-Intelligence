# Positive Product Narrative and Diagram Legibility Plan

**Adopted:** 28 August 2026  
**Applies to:** Parts 1-6, appendices, diagrams and future PDF editions

## Diagnosis

The manuscript overcorrected toward risk containment. Too many paragraphs explain what Spatial Catastrophe Risk Intelligence (SCRI) does not do before explaining the value it creates. This makes an Insurtech product sound like a compliance framework.

The preferred narrative order is:

1. State the product capability.
2. Show the user and practical outcome.
3. Explain how it works.
4. Explore trade-offs and failure scenarios.
5. State firm restrictions where they are genuinely necessary.

For example:

> SCRI gives claims teams an evidence-ranked queue, showing likely impact, uncertainty and supporting observations. Claims officers retain coverage and settlement authority.

This keeps the control while leading with utility.

## 1. Rebalance the narrative voice

Classify defensive statements into three groups:

- **Necessary controls:** legal authority, customer conduct, privacy, synthetic evidence, contractual triggers and model limitations. Keep these explicit.
- **Trade-offs worth exploring:** automation, continuous underwriting, crowd reliability, basis risk and intervention effectiveness. Develop these as balanced discussions.
- **Unnecessary defensive defaults:** repeated formulations such as "SCRI is not", "does not", "cannot" and "must not". Rewrite these around capability, workflow and accountable ownership.

Target balance:

- 70-80% constructive product and analytical narrative.
- 15-20% limitations, uncertainty and trade-offs.
- 5-10% firm restrictions in consequential areas.

## 2. Strengthen the founder and product voice

Each part should open and close around an operational proposition:

- What problem does an insurer experience?
- What does SCRI enable?
- Who uses the result?
- How does the workflow improve?
- What evidence demonstrates value?
- What remains to be tested?

Governance should support the product story rather than dominate it.

## 3. Explore defensive questions

Where risk is important, investigate it instead of merely closing it. For mid-event underwriting, explore:

- Which live signals legitimately improve accumulation management?
- Which signals belong in future renewal analysis?
- When does risk monitoring become unfair customer treatment?
- How should the interface separate operational intelligence from contractual action?
- What controls let insurers innovate confidently?

## 4. Redesign diagram sizing and density

Important diagrams must be comfortable to read at normal page view.

- Give important diagrams dedicated half-page or full-page layouts.
- Replace long horizontal flows with vertical or two-row structures.
- Split diagrams containing more than roughly 8-10 meaningful nodes.
- Increase Mermaid font size, node padding and line thickness.
- Shorten text inside nodes and move explanations into captions.
- Use landscape pages selectively for wide sequence or systems diagrams.
- Keep each sequence diagram to the roles needed for that workflow.
- Test at 100% PDF view and at physical Letter or A4 scale without zoom assistance.

**Acceptance test:** every label is comfortably readable at 100% view on a typical 13-15 inch screen.

## 5. Expand the Kenyan catastrophe stories

Add:

- A detailed Nzoia or lower-Tana flood reconstruction.
- A drought livelihood chronology across rainfall, pasture, livestock condition, milk, market access and household consumption.
- A locust movement-and-control reconstruction.
- A Mount Kenya fire detection, response and conditional-loss narrative.
- A West Pokot landslide and infrastructure-isolation scenario.
- A coastal heat and severe-weather livelihood narrative.

Each reconstruction should include event chronology, affected livelihoods, available observations, insurer exposure, claims pathways, financial consequences and the SCRI product experience.

## 6. Extend worked actuarial modelling

Build worked cases for:

- Flood property and crop portfolios.
- Drought livestock or livelihood products.
- Locust crop loss.
- Wildfire property, forestry or business interruption.
- Landslide road access and property accumulation.
- Heat and severe-storm business interruption.

Each case should show assumptions, exposure, vulnerability, policy terms, ground-up loss, insured loss, reinsurance, uncertainty and decision use.

## 7. Improve maps and analytical curves

Add:

- Basin and river-network maps.
- Historical event footprints.
- County or grid-level exposure overlays.
- Flood-depth or probability surfaces.
- Drought indicator histories.
- Claims-emergence curves.
- Vulnerability curves.
- Event-loss distributions.
- Legible OEP and AEP curves.
- Basis-risk and intervention-response comparisons.

Every map should state its source, date, spatial resolution and intended interpretation.

## 8. Develop the product-interface story

Expand the product journey:

```text
Event workspace
    -> Portfolio accumulation
    -> Policy and exposure drill-down
    -> Claims readiness
    -> Actuarial loss nowcast
    -> Reinsurance and liquidity
    -> Post-event learning and renewal
```

Each screen should answer a user question, show the minimum supporting evidence and identify the available action.

- Positive product-led narrative throughout.
- Defensive statements concentrated in appropriate sections.
- No diagram requires zoom to read.
- No duplicate captions, clipping or stranded paragraphs.
- Consistent figure numbering.
- Kenyan event evidence beside the relevant story.
- Synthetic and empirical material clearly distinguished.
- Full-page visual inspection at 100% scale.
